A modern historian remarks to a colleague that visiting a plain third-party mirror — an ad-free wrapper — is the easiest way to check what a public figure actually said without being assaulted by pre-roll ads, cookie walls, and autoplay chum.

Stop and ask what happens when that historian writes the footnote.

Under **MLA 9** you are instructed to cite *the platform copy you consulted* — "the copy of the video sitting on YouTube." If the historian cites the ad-laden URL they avoided, the footnote lies about their method. If they cite the quiet text mirror they actually read, a reviewer accuses them of citing a secondary scrape instead of the primary source.

> **Both styles confuse the window with the room.**

That is the whole problem, and the whole of `le.go` follows from it.

## The theorem

> **The citation is the event; the medium of consumption is the projection.**

When SikMadMan speaks the remix grant between `182s` and `202s` of *Open Arms*, **that utterance on its own clock is the event**. Watching it on `www.youtube.com` (authorial video surface), streaming it on `music.youtube.com` (catalogue inverse surface), reading it on `le.go/bare/` (zero-CSS, zero-JS, zero-ad HTML), or pasting it as a Chicago 18 footnote in a seminar paper are **four projections of one coordinate**.

Change your viewing angle whenever you want; the event does not move.

## Why it matters here

| Surface | What it is | Timestamp vocabulary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `youtube.com/watch?v=…` | authorial surface | `&t=` (playback time) |
| `music.youtube.com/watch?v=…` | catalogue / inverse surface | none — work-level only |
| `le.go/bare/#l-grant` | ad-free projection | `#fragment` (never sent to the server) |

Three syntaxes, two incompatible object ontologies, zero awareness that all three are **the same 20-second event**.
